Metric vs Imperial: The Two Measurement Systems Explained

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Measurement systems

Almost every measurement you will ever make belongs to one of two families: the metric system or the imperial system. They measure the same world — the same roads, the same bags of flour, the same summer afternoons — but they slice it up in completely different ways. Understanding how each system is built makes converting between them far less mysterious.

How the metric system is built

The metric system, formally the International System of Units (SI), is built on powers of ten. Each quantity has one base unit — the metre for length, the kilogram for mass, the litre for volume — and every larger or smaller unit is that base multiplied or divided by 10, 100 or 1,000. A kilometre is 1,000 metres; a centimetre is one hundredth of a metre; a millilitre is one thousandth of a litre.

The prefixes do the heavy lifting: milli- means a thousandth, centi- a hundredth, kilo- a thousand. Learn a handful of prefixes and you can read almost any metric unit on sight, even one you have never used before. The units also connect to each other neatly: a litre is exactly the volume of a cube 10 cm on each side, and a kilogram was historically tied to the mass of a litre of water.

How the imperial system is built

The imperial system grew out of centuries of British trade and custom rather than a single design — a story told in full in our history of measurement units. Its units relate to each other by irregular steps that were once convenient in daily life: 12 inches in a foot, 3 feet in a yard, 1,760 yards in a mile; 16 ounces in a pound, 14 pounds in a stone; 8 pints in a gallon. None of these numbers is wrong — they are simply historical. A foot was roughly a foot, an inch roughly a thumb's width, and the system accumulated over time.

The United States uses a closely related set called US customary units. They share most names with imperial units — inch, foot, pound — but a few differ, most famously volume: a US gallon is 3.785 litres while an imperial gallon is 4.546 litres. That difference catches people out in recipes and fuel-economy figures to this day.

Where each system is used

Metric is the official system in the overwhelming majority of the world and is the standard language of science, medicine and engineering everywhere, including in the United States. Imperial and US customary units remain in everyday use in the US for road distances, body weight and cooking, and linger in the UK in a hybrid form: miles on road signs and pints in pubs, but grams and litres in shops.

Which system is "better"?

For calculation, metric wins easily: converting within the system is just moving a decimal point, and the units interlock. For everyday intuition, the honest answer is whichever system you grew up with. Someone raised on Fahrenheit feels that 85 °F is a hot day without converting anything; someone raised on Celsius feels the same about 29 °C. Neither intuition is more correct — both are learned.

The key conversion anchors

If you only memorise a few bridges between the systems, make it these: 1 inch = 2.54 cm exactly, 1 mile ≈ 1.609 km, 1 pound ≈ 453.6 grams, and 1 litre ≈ 0.264 US gallons. With those four anchors, the method in our guide to how to convert units, and the converter at the top of this site, you can move between the two systems fluently — which, in a world that uses both, is the skill that actually matters.

How to Convert Units: A Simple Step-by-Step Method

UnitConverterHub · 5 min read · How-to

Unit conversion looks like a pile of unrelated facts — 2.54 here, 1.609 there, 453.6 somewhere else. In reality almost every conversion follows one simple method. Learn the method once and you can convert anything, including units you have never seen before, with nothing more than a single known relationship.

Step 1: Find the relationship

Every conversion starts from one true statement linking the two units, such as "1 mile = 1.609344 kilometres" or "1 inch = 2.54 centimetres". You only ever need the relationship for a single unit of the thing you are converting from. Reference tables, textbooks and the quick reference table on our converter all give relationships in exactly this form.

Step 2: Decide whether to multiply or divide

This is where most mistakes happen, and there is a reliable way to think about it: converting into a smaller unit means you will have more of them, so you multiply. Converting into a bigger unit means fewer of them, so you divide. Kilometres to metres? Metres are smaller, the number grows — multiply by 1,000. Grams to kilograms? Kilograms are bigger, the number shrinks — divide by 1,000.

Step 3: Do the calculation

Work through a full example. Convert 5 miles to kilometres. The relationship is 1 mile = 1.609344 km. Kilometres are the smaller unit here (more of them fit in the same distance), so multiply: 5 × 1.609344 = 8.04672 km. To reverse it, divide: 8.04672 ÷ 1.609344 = 5 miles exactly.

The unit-factor shortcut: write the relationship as a fraction that equals 1 — for example (1.609344 km / 1 mile) — and multiply your value by it. The old unit cancels out on paper, leaving only the new one. If the units don't cancel, you have the fraction upside down.

Step 4: Sanity-check the answer

Before trusting a result, ask whether its size makes sense. A person cannot weigh 4,500 kilograms or stand 0.0018 metres tall. Most conversion errors are decimal-point slips or multiply-instead-of-divide mistakes, and both produce answers that are wildly too big or too small — exactly the kind a two-second sanity check catches.

Converting in two hops

Sometimes there is no direct relationship handy, but there is a path through a middle unit. Stones to grams, for example: 1 stone = 14 pounds, and 1 pound = 453.59237 grams, so 1 stone = 14 × 453.59237 = 6,350.29 grams. Chained conversions multiply their factors together. This is also exactly how our converter works internally: every unit in a category is defined against one base unit, and any conversion is a trip down to the base and back up. If the factors themselves feel arbitrary, our explainer on the metric and imperial systems shows where they came from.

The exception: temperature

Temperature is the famous exception to the multiply-or-divide rule, because its scales have different zero points as well as different step sizes. Celsius to Fahrenheit needs a multiplication and an addition: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. Our temperature conversion guide walks through why, and the converter above handles it with the full formula rather than a single factor.

A quick practice set

  • 12 inches → centimetres: 12 × 2.54 = 30.48 cm
  • 2.5 kg → grams: 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 g
  • 60 mph → km/h: 60 × 1.609344 = 96.56 km/h
  • 500 ml → litres: 500 ÷ 1,000 = 0.5 L

Once the method feels natural, the individual factors stop being trivia to memorise and become what they really are: just the first step of a process you already understand.

Temperature Conversion: Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Temperature

Temperature is the one everyday measurement where conversion takes more than a single multiplication — the famous exception to the simple method in our guide to how to convert units. The reason is simple once you see it: the Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales do not share a zero point, and two of them do not even share a step size. This guide explains each scale, gives the exact formulas, and shares the mental shortcuts worth knowing.

The three scales at a glance

Celsius (°C) is anchored to water: 0 °C is its freezing point and 100 °C its boiling point at standard pressure. It is the everyday scale in most of the world and the working scale of science alongside Kelvin.

Fahrenheit (°F) places water's freezing point at 32 °F and boiling at 212 °F — a span of 180 degrees, which means each Fahrenheit degree is only 5/9 the size of a Celsius degree. It remains the everyday scale in the United States.

Kelvin (K) starts at absolute zero, the coldest temperature physically possible, so it has no negative values. Its steps are the same size as Celsius degrees, which makes it the natural scale for physics: 0 K = −273.15 °C.

The exact formulas

ConversionFormula
Celsius → Fahrenheit°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32
Fahrenheit → Celsius°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9
Celsius → KelvinK = °C + 273.15
Kelvin → Celsius°C = K − 273.15
Fahrenheit → KelvinK = (°F − 32) × 5/9 + 273.15
Kelvin → Fahrenheit°F = (K − 273.15) × 9/5 + 32

Notice the pattern: converting to or from Kelvin is a single shift of 273.15 once you are in Celsius, because the two scales share a step size. Only Fahrenheit needs the 9/5 or 5/9 scaling as well.

Worked examples

A comfortable room at 21 °C: (21 × 9/5) + 32 = 37.8 + 32 = 69.8 °F. A hot oven at 400 °F: (400 − 32) × 5/9 = 368 × 5/9 = 204.4 °C. Human body temperature, 37 °C, is 98.6 °F — and in Kelvin, 37 + 273.15 = 310.15 K.

Reference points worth memorising

  • −40 is the crossover point: −40 °C = −40 °F exactly.
  • 0 °C = 32 °F — water freezes.
  • 20 °C = 68 °F — a mild day.
  • 37 °C = 98.6 °F — normal body temperature.
  • 100 °C = 212 °F — water boils.
  • 180 °C = 356 °F — a typical baking temperature (often rounded to 350 °F in US recipes; our cooking conversions guide covers more kitchen temperatures).

A mental shortcut for weather

For a fast estimate, double the Celsius temperature and add 30: 25 °C → 50 + 30 = 80 °F, close to the exact 77 °F. Going the other way, subtract 30 from Fahrenheit and halve it. The shortcut drifts by a few degrees at the extremes, but for deciding whether you need a jacket it is more than good enough — and for anything precise, the converter at the top of this page uses the exact formulas.

Why Kelvin has no degree symbol

Kelvin is written without the ° symbol — just "300 K", never "300 °K" — because it is an absolute scale rather than a relative one. Degrees measure distance from an arbitrary zero; kelvins measure an actual quantity of thermal energy from true zero. It is a small typographic detail that signals a genuinely different kind of scale.

Cooking Conversions: Cups, Grams and Millilitres

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Cooking

Every home cook eventually meets a recipe written in the "wrong" units: an American recipe in cups when your kitchen works in grams, or a European one in millilitres when your measuring jug shows fluid ounces. That split is the everyday legacy of the metric and imperial systems. Cooking conversions have one extra complication that pure unit conversion does not — for dry ingredients, weight depends on what is in the cup. This guide explains how to handle all of it.

Volume is easy: cups to millilitres

For liquids, conversion is exact because a cup is a volume and a millilitre is a volume. One US cup = 236.588 ml, usually rounded to 240 ml in recipes. A tablespoon is 14.79 ml (rounded to 15 ml) and a teaspoon 4.93 ml (rounded to 5 ml). Water, milk, oil and stock all convert with these numbers directly.

One caution: the Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, not 15 ml, and the metric cup used in Australia and New Zealand is 250 ml. For most recipes the difference is small, but in baking — where ratios matter — it is worth checking the recipe's country of origin.

Weight is trickier: cups to grams

A cup measures volume, a gram measures mass, and the bridge between them is density — which is different for every ingredient. A cup of water weighs about 237 g, but a cup of sifted flour weighs only about 120 g, and a cup of granulated sugar about 200 g. There is no single cups-to-grams factor; there is one per ingredient.

Why bakers prefer grams

A cup of flour can vary by 30 grams or more depending on whether it was scooped, spooned, sifted or packed — a swing large enough to turn a light cake dense. Weighing removes that guesswork entirely: 125 g of flour is 125 g however it got into the bowl. That repeatability is why professional bakers work in grams and baker's percentages, and why a small kitchen scale is the single best upgrade for consistent baking.

Oven temperatures

The other conversion every cross-border recipe needs: 350 °F, the most common US baking temperature, is 176.7 °C — usually written as 175 °C or 180 °C in metric recipes. A moderate oven of 180 °C is 356 °F. For fan (convection) ovens, reduce the stated temperature by about 20 °C / 25 °F. Our temperature guide covers the formulas in full, and the converter above handles any exact value.

A practical workflow

  1. Convert liquids with volume-to-volume numbers (cups → ml).
  2. Convert dry ingredients with a per-ingredient weight table (cups → g), or weigh as you go.
  3. Convert the oven temperature last, and round to the nearest mark your oven actually offers.
  4. When in doubt, round gently: cooking tolerates small errors everywhere except baking ratios and oven heat.

With those rules, any recipe in the world becomes usable in your kitchen — which is exactly what a good conversion should do.

Why the World Uses Different Units: A Short History

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · History

It can feel absurd that humanity measures the same planet with incompatible rulers. But units were never designed as a global system — they were local tools, invented again and again wherever people built, traded and farmed. The variety we live with today is the fossil record of that history.

Units from the human body

The earliest units were the ones always at hand. The cubit measured from elbow to fingertip and built the pyramids. The foot was a foot, the inch a thumb's width (its name traces to the Latin uncia, a twelfth part — hence twelve inches to the foot), and the yard was linked in legend to a king's outstretched arm. Body-based units were wonderfully portable and hopelessly inconsistent: your foot and my foot differ, and so did every town's.

Trade forces standards

As trade widened, inconsistency became expensive. A bushel of grain that changed size at the county border invited cheating and quarrels. Monarchs and cities responded with official standards — physical objects kept under guard, like the standard yard bar in London — and laws tying local measures to them. Britain's imperial system, formalised in 1824, was one such tidying-up: a single set of gallons, pounds and yards for an empire. The United States, having inherited the older British measures before that reform, kept its own versions — which is why the US and imperial gallons still differ today.

The metric revolution

Revolutionary France attempted something bolder: throw the patchwork away and rebuild measurement from nature itself. In the 1790s the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator through Paris, surveyed by astronomers over six gruelling years. The kilogram was the mass of a litre of water. The system was decimal, universal and — in principle — belonged to no king. Adoption was slow at first, even in France, but the logic was irresistible to scientists and, eventually, to governments: by the mid-20th century most of the world had gone metric.

Why some places held out

The United States considered metric adoption repeatedly — Thomas Jefferson studied the French system, and the US legally defined its customary units in metric terms in 1893 — but the cost of converting road signs, machinery, tooling and habits always outweighed the benefit in the short term. Britain began metrication in 1965 and completed it in shops, but kept miles and pints where the public resisted. The pattern is the same everywhere: units change when the practical gain beats the practical pain, and not before.

Units defined by physics itself

The modern ending to the story is elegant. In 2019 the SI system was redefined so that every base unit rests on a fixed constant of nature — the speed of light, the Planck constant, the charge of the electron. The kilogram is no longer a metal cylinder in a Paris vault slowly changing mass; it is a definition any advanced laboratory can realise from first principles. Measurement, which began with elbows and thumbs, now begins with the constants of the universe.

Living with two systems

For the foreseeable future, daily life will stay bilingual in units: kilometres beside miles, grams beside ounces. That is less a failure than a reflection of how deeply measurement is woven into habit and infrastructure. The practical skill is fluency — knowing the anchors between the metric and imperial systems and converting confidently when it counts, which is exactly what our step-by-step guide to how to convert units teaches. That is the whole reason this site exists.

How to Convert Kilograms to Pounds (Plus a Mental Shortcut)

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Weight

Kilograms to pounds is probably the single most common conversion between the metric and imperial systems. Body weight, luggage allowances, gym plates, parcels, pets, produce — the world weighs things in kilograms, except where it weighs them in pounds, and the two meet constantly. The good news is that this conversion has both an exact factor worth knowing and a mental shortcut so good that you will rarely need anything else.

The exact conversion

One kilogram equals 2.20462 pounds. Going the other way, one pound equals 0.453592 kilograms — the pound is defined internationally as exactly 0.45359237 kg, so these are not rough figures but the actual definitions rounded for display. To convert kilograms to pounds, multiply by 2.20462. To convert pounds to kilograms, multiply by 0.453592 (or divide by 2.20462). Our weight converter does this instantly for any value, but the arithmetic is simple enough to follow by hand.

The ×2.2 shortcut

For mental maths, multiply by 2.2 instead of 2.20462. How much accuracy do you lose? The shortcut factor is about 0.21% smaller than the exact one, so your answer comes out roughly a fifth of one percent low. On a 70 kg person that is the difference between 154 lb (shortcut) and 154.32 lb (exact) — a third of a pound, far less than the amount your weight naturally moves during a single day. For luggage, shopping, body weight and the gym, nobody can tell the difference.

The double-and-add-a-tenth trick

Multiplying by 2.2 has an even friendlier form: double the kilograms, then add a tenth of the doubled number. For 70 kg: double it to 140, add a tenth of that (14), and you get 154 lb. For 5 kg: double to 10, add 1, get 11 lb. Each step is trivial mental arithmetic — doubling and moving a decimal point — yet the result is the full ×2.2 answer. Going the other way works too: to turn pounds into kilograms mentally, halve the pounds and subtract a tenth of the half. For 150 lb: half is 75, minus 7.5 gives 67.5 kg, close to the exact 68.04 kg (this is the ×0.45 approximation, about 0.8% low).

Worked examples

  • 5 kg — a large bag of flour, or a small cat: 5 × 2.20462 = 11.02 lb (shortcut: 11 lb).
  • 70 kg — around the average adult: 70 × 2.20462 = 154.32 lb (shortcut: 154 lb).
  • 90 kg — a heavy suitcase problem or a large adult: 90 × 2.20462 = 198.42 lb (shortcut: 198 lb).
  • 23 kg — a common checked-baggage limit: 23 × 2.20462 = 50.7 lb, which is why so many airlines pair a 23 kg allowance with a 50 lb one.

When precision matters — and when it doesn't

Use the exact factor, or the converter, whenever a number has consequences. Medicine is dosed by body weight, and a child's dose calculated from a rounded weight can drift in the wrong direction. Freight and postal shipping charge by weight bands, so a parcel near a limit deserves an exact figure. Aviation weight-and-balance calculations and competitive weigh-ins (boxing, weightlifting, rowing) are likewise no place for approximations. The same goes for trade: if you are buying or selling by weight, use the definition, not the shortcut.

For everything else — tracking your own weight, checking a suitcase against a limit with room to spare, estimating a dog's weight for a food packet, reading a foreign recipe — the ×2.2 shortcut is entirely fine. The honest rule is that the shortcut's 0.2% error is almost always smaller than the error in the measurement itself: bathroom scales, luggage scales and gym plates are rarely accurate to a fifth of a percent anyway.

The one mistake to avoid

The commonest error with this pair is not the factor but the direction: people divide when they should multiply. The sanity check from our guide to how to convert units applies — pounds are the smaller unit, so the pounds number is always the bigger number, roughly double. If your answer in pounds is smaller than your kilograms, you have gone the wrong way. Keep that picture in mind and kilograms-to-pounds becomes one of the friendliest conversions there is.

Miles to Kilometres Explained: Road Distances Without the Guesswork

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Length

Drive across a border, rent a car abroad, follow a foreign training plan or watch an imported car's dashboard, and you will meet the mile–kilometre problem within the hour. The two units divide up every road on Earth between them, and switching fluently is mostly a matter of one exact factor, one good approximation and one surprisingly elegant trick.

The exact numbers

One mile is exactly 1.609344 kilometres — the international mile is defined as 1,609.344 metres, so this figure is exact, not measured. In reverse, one kilometre is 0.621371 miles. To convert miles to kilometres, multiply by 1.609344; to convert kilometres to miles, multiply by 0.621371 (or divide by 1.609344). The length converter at the top of this site handles any value instantly; what follows is how to manage without it.

The everyday rule: 1.6

For mental arithmetic, 1.6 is close enough for almost everything. Multiply miles by 1.6 to get kilometres (60 miles ≈ 96 km, against the exact 96.56), or divide kilometres by 1.6 to get miles (100 km ≈ 62.5 miles, against the exact 62.14). The error is well under one percent — invisible on a road trip, where the distance to the next town matters more than its third decimal place.

The Fibonacci shortcut

Here is the elegant trick. The Fibonacci sequence — 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34 — has consecutive numbers whose ratio is about 1.618, very close to the mile–kilometre factor of 1.609. That coincidence gives you instant conversions at the most common sizes: 5 miles ≈ 8 km, 8 miles ≈ 13 km, 13 miles ≈ 21 km. Run it backwards for kilometres to miles: 8 km ≈ 5 miles, 13 km ≈ 8 miles, 21 km ≈ 13 miles. The approximation is within about half a percent, and it explains a happy accident of road running: a 5 km race is 3.1 miles, and an 8 km run is almost exactly 5 miles.

Speed limits and road speeds

Distance is only half the story — the same factor converts the speeds on the signs, which is why our speed converter uses it too. The limits you will meet most often:

  • 30 mph ≈ 48 km/h — the built-up-area limit in both systems' home countries (exact: 48.28).
  • 50 mph ≈ 80 km/h — a common rural limit (exact: 80.47).
  • 60 mph ≈ 97 km/h — single-carriageway cruising (exact: 96.56).
  • 70 mph ≈ 113 km/h — the UK motorway limit, close to the 110–120 km/h motorway limits common elsewhere (exact: 112.65).
  • 100 km/h ≈ 62 mph — the round-number motorway limit in much of the world.

If you are driving a car whose speedometer shows only one unit, the pair worth memorising is the local limit in both: know that 50 km/h through a town is 31 mph, and that a 65 mph highway limit is about 105 km/h. Our article on speed units goes further, into knots and metres per second.

Famous distances in both units

The marathon is the best-known: 26.2 miles, which is 42.195 km — the kilometre figure is the official one, standardised for the 1908 London Olympics course and adopted worldwide. A "century" cycling ride is 100 miles, or about 161 km, while its metric cousin the 100 km ride is 62 miles. The classic British day trip, Land's End to John o' Groats, is about 874 miles — roughly 1,407 km. And the United States' Route 66 ran about 2,400 miles, some 3,860 km, a number that sounds epic in either unit.

Which direction trips people up

The classic error is treating the conversion as symmetric — assuming that if 10 miles is 16 km, then 10 km must also be 16 miles. Kilometres are the smaller unit, so there are always more of them: any distance has a bigger number in kilometres than in miles, by that factor of about 1.6. If your conversion made the number smaller going to kilometres, you divided when you should have multiplied. With the exact factor for precision, 1.6 for the road, and Fibonacci for the sizes that come up again and again, you have the whole conversion covered.

Hectares and Acres: Land Area Units Explained

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Area

Land is where the two measurement systems refuse to meet halfway. Houses are sold in square feet or square metres, but fields, farms, forests and estates are measured in two much bigger units that belong to different worlds: the hectare, metric and tidy, and the acre, ancient and stubbornly alive. If you ever read a land listing, a farming report or a news story about wildfires from another country, you will need to translate between them.

The numbers

One hectare is 10,000 square metres — a square 100 metres on each side. In imperial terms, one hectare equals 2.47105 acres. Going the other way, one acre is 4,046.86 square metres, which is 0.404686 hectares. To convert hectares to acres, multiply by 2.47105; to convert acres to hectares, multiply by 0.404686 (0.4 is a fine mental approximation, about one percent low). The area converter on this site covers both, along with square metres, square feet and square miles.

Picturing the sizes

An acre is 43,560 square feet, or 4,840 square yards. The most useful visual: an American football field's playing area between the goal lines is 100 yards by 53⅓ yards — about 1.10 acres — so an acre is roughly 91% of that playing area. Include the end zones and the whole field is about 1.32 acres. For the world's other football: a standard soccer pitch at 105 × 68 metres covers 7,140 square metres, which is 0.714 hectares, or about 1.76 acres. Turning that around, one hectare is about 1.4 soccer pitches — a handy image, because a hectare is otherwise hard to see in the mind's eye.

Where the acre came from

The acre is one of the oldest units still in daily use. In medieval England it meant the amount of land a team of oxen could plough in a single day — a definition based on work, not geometry. In practice it settled into a long, narrow strip: one furlong (660 feet, the length of a furrow) by one chain (66 feet) wide. Long strips suited ploughing, because turning a team of oxen around was the slow part. Multiply 660 by 66 and you get the acre's 43,560 square feet — an awkward number that is really a fossil of how fields were actually farmed. The hectare, by contrast, was designed: the "are" was set at 100 square metres in revolutionary France, and a hect-are is simply a hundred of them.

Where each unit is used today

Hectares are the working land unit across most of the world: European land registries and farm subsidies, forestry and conservation statistics, agriculture reporting from Australia to Brazil, and international figures such as deforestation and fire damage are all in hectares. Acres dominate in the United States, where farms, ranches and rural real estate are almost always described in acres, and they remain common in British and Irish farm sales, often listed alongside hectares. Canada and Australia use hectares officially, though older rural listings in acres still circulate. For homes rather than land, the units shrink — see our guide to square feet and square metres.

Working examples

  • A small farm of 40 hectares is 40 × 2.47105 = 98.8 acres.
  • A ranch of 500 acres is 500 × 0.404686 = 202.3 hectares.
  • A woodland of 2.5 hectares is about 6.2 acres.
  • A wildfire reported at 10,000 hectares has burned 24,710 acres — about 100 square kilometres, since 100 hectares make exactly one square kilometre.

That last relationship is worth keeping: hectares bridge neatly to square kilometres (divide by 100), while acres bridge to square miles (640 acres to one square mile). Each unit sits comfortably inside its own system; the conversion factors above are the bridge between them, and for anything beyond a rough picture, the area converter will do the arithmetic exactly.

Data Sizes Explained: Kilobytes, Megabytes, Gigabytes and Terabytes

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Data storage

Everyone has had the same small moment of suspicion: you buy a 500 GB drive, plug it in, and the computer insists it holds about 465. Nothing has been stolen and the drive is not faulty — you have simply run into the fact that data sizes are counted in two different ways at once. This guide explains both systems, the bits-versus-bytes trap, and what file sizes actually look like in daily life.

Bits and bytes

The smallest unit of data is the bit: a single 0 or 1. Eight bits grouped together make one byte, which is enough to store one character of text. From the byte upwards, everything scales: kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes, terabytes, each a step up from the last. The crucial habit to learn is that file sizes are measured in bytes, but connection speeds are measured in bits — a difference of a factor of eight that catches people out constantly, and which we return to below. Our data storage converter works in bytes and bits across all of these units.

The two counting systems

Here is the source of the confusion. In the decimal system — the one storage manufacturers use, and the one this site's converter uses — each step is a thousand: 1 KB = 1,000 bytes, 1 MB = 1,000 KB, 1 GB = 1,000 MB, 1 TB = 1,000 GB. This follows the ordinary meaning of kilo-, mega- and giga- everywhere else in science.

Computers, however, think in powers of two, and 2¹⁰ = 1,024 is conveniently close to 1,000. So operating systems traditionally counted in steps of 1,024: 1 KiB (kibibyte) = 1,024 bytes, 1 MiB (mebibyte) = 1,024 KiB, 1 GiB (gibibyte) = 1,024 MiB. The special names — kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte — exist precisely to keep the two systems apart, though many programs still display binary quantities while labelling them with the decimal names.

Why your 500 GB drive "shrinks"

The manufacturer counts decimally: 500 GB = 500,000,000,000 bytes, honestly delivered. Your operating system counts in binary: 500,000,000,000 ÷ 1,073,741,824 (the number of bytes in a GiB) = about 465.7 GiB, which it displays as roughly "465 GB". The gap grows with size, because the 1,000-vs-1,024 difference compounds at every step: about 2.4% at the kilobyte scale, 7.4% at gigabytes, and nearly 10% at terabytes. A small amount of space also goes to formatting and file-system structures, but the counting systems explain almost all of the "missing" capacity.

How big is everyday data?

  • A plain text email: a few KB.
  • A page of a document or a simple web image: tens to hundreds of KB.
  • A phone photo: roughly 3–5 MB.
  • A song as an MP3 or stream rip: roughly 3–10 MB, depending on length and quality.
  • A phone app: anywhere from a few MB to several GB for large games.
  • An HD film: several GB; a 4K film can run to 15–25 GB or more.
  • A modern console or PC game: 50–150 GB is now unremarkable.

Speeds in bits, files in bytes

Broadband and mobile connections are sold in megabits per second (Mbps). Files are sized in megabytes (MB). Since a byte is eight bits, divide the advertised speed by eight to get the fastest possible download rate in megabytes per second: a 100 Mbps connection can theoretically move about 12.5 MB per second. So a 5 GB game update (5,000 MB) on that connection takes at least 5,000 ÷ 12.5 = 400 seconds — around seven minutes — and longer in practice. If a download ever seems eight times slower than the speed you pay for, this factor of eight is almost certainly why. It is one of the classic errors in our roundup of common conversion mistakes.

Which system should you use?

When buying storage, expect decimal numbers on the box and binary numbers on the screen, and do not treat the difference as lost space. When a program or standard uses KiB, MiB and GiB labels, it is being precise about binary counting. And when you convert with the data converter on this site, both families are listed separately — KB, MB, GB and TB in decimal, KiB, MiB, GiB and TiB in binary — so you can translate between the label on the box and the number on your screen with confidence.

Speed Units Explained: mph, km/h and Knots

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Speed

Speed is measured in more competing units than almost anything else in daily life. Road signs speak miles per hour in some countries and kilometres per hour in most others; boats and aircraft use a third unit, the knot, that confuses everyone who meets it for the first time; and scientists quietly use a fourth, metres per second. Here is how they all connect.

The exact relationships

  • 1 mph = 1.609344 km/h — the mile–kilometre factor applied to speed.
  • 1 knot = 1.852 km/h = 1.15078 mph — one nautical mile per hour.
  • 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h — and conversely, divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s.
  • 1 km/h = 0.621371 mph.

Any of these can be chained: to turn knots into mph, go knots → km/h (×1.852) → mph (×0.621371), or use the direct figure of 1.15078. The speed converter on this site handles all of them, including feet per second.

Why sailors and pilots use knots

The knot is not an eccentricity — it is geometry. A nautical mile is defined as exactly 1,852 metres, chosen because it equals one minute of latitude: one sixtieth of a degree around the Earth. Navigators plot positions in degrees and minutes on charts, so distances measured in nautical miles read directly off the chart's latitude scale with no conversion at all. Speed in knots is then just nautical miles per hour, and the whole system stays consistent from chart to logbook. Aviation inherited the practice from maritime navigation, which is why pilots still file speeds in knots and distances in nautical miles even though they fly over land. The name itself comes from an older technology: sailors measured speed by paying out a rope with knots tied at regular intervals and counting how many passed through their hands in a timed interval — the knots were literal.

Quick rules for the road

For driving, two rules cover nearly everything: mph to km/h, multiply by 1.6; km/h to mph, multiply by 0.62 (or divide by 1.6). So 50 mph ≈ 80 km/h, and 110 km/h ≈ 68 mph. These are the same approximations as for distance — our miles to kilometres guide explains why they work and adds the Fibonacci shortcut for common values. For knots, a rough rule is "double it, then take a little off" to get km/h: 20 knots ≈ 37 km/h, and the doubling gives you 40 before the correction.

Speeds in context

  • Walking: about 5 km/h (3.1 mph, 1.4 m/s).
  • Cycling, unhurried: 15–20 km/h (9–12 mph).
  • City driving: 30–50 km/h (19–31 mph).
  • Highway driving: 100–120 km/h (62–75 mph).
  • A large ship: 20–25 knots (37–46 km/h) — slower than a bicycle commute is not, but close.
  • High-speed train: up to about 300 km/h (186 mph) in service.
  • Airliner cruising: about 900 km/h (560 mph, roughly 485 knots).
  • The speed of sound at sea level: about 1,235 km/h (767 mph).

Where metres per second rule

In physics, engineering and athletics analysis, metres per second is the standard because it plugs straight into formulas with metres and seconds. The conversion is the friendliest of all: multiply m/s by 3.6 for km/h, divide km/h by 3.6 for m/s. A sprinter at 10 m/s is travelling at 36 km/h; a strong wind at 25 m/s is a 90 km/h gale. Wind speeds in weather reports, incidentally, are another place units ambush the unwary — some countries report km/h, others mph, and aviation and shipping use knots. Whichever unit a figure arrives in, the relationships above will get it into the one you think in.

Tyre Pressure Units: PSI, Bar and kPa Explained

UnitConverterHub · 5 min read · Pressure

Few conversions matter for safety as directly as this one. The gauge at the petrol station speaks one unit, your car's door sticker may speak another, and the manual a third — and a tyre inflated to the right number in the wrong unit is badly wrong. Fortunately the three pressure units used for tyres convert cleanly, and the numbers for a typical car are easy to remember in all of them.

The three units and their factors

PSI (pounds per square inch) is the everyday unit in the United States and is still common in the UK. Bar is the unit across most of Europe — one bar is 100,000 pascals, very close to standard atmospheric pressure (1.013 bar). kPa (kilopascals) is the strict SI unit, printed on many modern door stickers and manuals. The exact relationships:

  • 1 bar = 100 kPa = 14.5038 PSI
  • 1 PSI = 6.89476 kPa (and about 0.0689 bar)
  • 1 kPa = 0.145038 PSI

Bar to kPa is the easiest conversion in this whole article — just multiply by 100. For the others, the pressure converter on this site gives exact values, and also covers atmospheres and millimetres of mercury.

Typical car tyre pressures

Most ordinary cars run their tyres at 30–35 PSI, which is about 2.1–2.4 bar, or 210–240 kPa. A very common specification, 32 PSI, is 2.21 bar or 221 kPa. If you remember just one anchor — 2.2 bar ≈ 32 PSI ≈ 220 kPa — you can sanity-check any gauge in any unit. Heavily loaded cars and rear tyres sometimes take a few PSI more; the sticker usually lists both normal and full-load figures.

Where to find your car's correct pressure

The recommended pressure is set by the car maker, not the tyre maker, and you will find it in three places: the sticker on the driver's door jamb (or door pillar), the inside of the fuel filler flap on many European cars, and the owner's manual. One warning that matters: the number moulded into the tyre's sidewall is the maximum pressure the tyre can safely hold, not the recommended pressure for your car. Inflating to the sidewall maximum — often 44 or 51 PSI — over-inflates the tyre for normal use. Always use the car maker's figure.

Why the right pressure matters

Under-inflated tyres flex too much in the sidewall, which builds up heat, wears the shoulders of the tread, lengthens braking distances and increases fuel consumption — and sustained under-inflation is a leading cause of blowouts. Over-inflated tyres shrink the contact patch, reducing grip, wearing the centre of the tread and giving a harsh ride. Pressure also drifts on its own: tyres naturally lose roughly 1 PSI a month, and pressure falls about 1 PSI for every 5–6 °C drop in temperature, which is why tyre-pressure warning lights often appear on the first cold morning of autumn. Check monthly, and always when the tyres are cold — driving heats the air inside and inflates the reading.

Bicycles, trucks and other tyres

The ranges elsewhere are wider than cars. A road bicycle tyre runs 80–120 PSI (5.5–8.3 bar) — far higher than a car, because the tyre is narrow and the contact patch tiny — while a mountain bike tyre uses just 25–40 PSI (1.7–2.8 bar) for grip on loose ground. Truck and bus tyres are typically around 100 PSI (about 6.9 bar) and above, carrying far greater loads. Wheelbarrows, trailers and caravan tyres each have their own specifications; in every case the method is the same — find the vehicle maker's recommended figure, convert it into the unit your gauge shows using the factors above or the pressure converter, and check while cold.

Fuel Economy Explained: MPG vs Litres per 100 km

UnitConverterHub · 7 min read · Fuel economy

An American review praises a car for returning 40 MPG. A European brochure describes what sounds like the same car as using 5.9 litres per 100 km. A British magazine quotes 48 MPG for it. All three figures can describe identical efficiency — because fuel economy is measured in two opposite ways, using two different gallons. Untangling it takes one formula and one warning.

Two systems that measure in opposite directions

Miles per gallon (MPG) measures distance per unit of fuel: how far a gallon takes you. Higher is better. Litres per 100 kilometres (L/100 km) measures fuel per unit of distance: how much you burn covering a fixed 100 km. Lower is better. They are reciprocals of each other (with unit conversions folded in), like the difference between measuring a runner by speed or by pace per kilometre. This opposition is the first trap: a "bigger" fuel economy number is good news in America and bad news in Europe.

The gallon trap: US and UK MPG are not the same

The second trap is the gallon itself. A US gallon is 3.78541 litres; an imperial (UK) gallon is 4.54609 litres — about 20% larger, a story told in full in our article on litres and gallons. Because a UK gallon contains more fuel, the same car travels further on it, so its UK MPG figure is always about 20% higher than its US MPG figure. A car rated 40 MPG in the United States would be advertised at roughly 48 MPG in Britain without using a drop less fuel. Never compare MPG figures across the Atlantic without checking which gallon produced them.

The conversion formulas

Because the two measures are reciprocals, the conversion is a division, not a multiplication:

  • L/100 km = 235.215 ÷ MPG (US gallons)
  • L/100 km = 282.481 ÷ MPG (imperial gallons)
  • MPG (US) = 235.215 ÷ L/100 km
  • MPG (imperial) = 282.481 ÷ L/100 km

The constants are not magic: 235.215 combines the US gallon in litres (3.78541) with 100 kilometres expressed in miles. Memorise 235 for American figures and 282 for British ones and you can convert any fuel economy number on the spot. If gallons themselves are the problem — a tank size quoted in one system, a fuel price in another — our volume converter translates litres and both gallons directly.

Worked examples

  • 30 MPG (US): 235.215 ÷ 30 = 7.8 L/100 km — a typical petrol family car.
  • 40 MPG (US): 235.215 ÷ 40 = 5.9 L/100 km — an efficient modern hatchback.
  • 50 MPG (imperial): 282.481 ÷ 50 = 5.6 L/100 km — and note this equals only about 41.6 MPG in US terms.
  • 8 L/100 km: 235.215 ÷ 8 = 29 MPG (US), or 282.481 ÷ 8 = 35 MPG (imperial).
  • 5 L/100 km: 47 MPG (US) or 56 MPG (imperial) — hybrid territory.

Reading figures sensibly

Two more cautions. First, official figures come from laboratory test cycles that differ by region — the American EPA cycle and the European WLTP cycle produce different results for the same car even after the units are converted, so converted comparisons are approximate. Second, L/100 km makes real-world differences easier to judge at the efficient end: improving from 10 to 8 L/100 km saves far more fuel over a year than improving from 5 to 4, even though both are "two better" or "one better" steps on their scales — MPG hides this, because its scale stretches as numbers rise. Whichever system your country uses, convert carefully, state the gallon, and the numbers will behave.

Shoe and Clothing Size Conversions: US, UK and EU

UnitConverterHub · 7 min read · Sizes

Order shoes from another continent and you meet a peculiar kind of unit problem: your foot has not changed, but it is suddenly a US 9, a UK 8 and an EU 42½ at the same time. Clothing is no better — a medium here is a large there, and a size 12 in London is a size 8 in New York. These are not measurement units in the strict sense, but labels from three independent systems, and converting between them needs both the rough rules and one reliable method.

Why the systems differ

Each system grew from its own tradition. British and American shoe sizes step upward in "barleycorns" — thirds of an inch — from a starting point set in the middle ages, but the two systems start counting from slightly different places and split men's, women's and children's scales differently. European sizes use the "Paris point", a step of two-thirds of a centimetre, counted from zero at no length at all — which is why EU numbers are so much larger. None of the systems was designed to match the others, and manufacturers add their own variation on top, because a size is ultimately stamped on a manufacturer's last (the mould a shoe is built around), and lasts differ.

Shoe size relationships

The working rules for adults are these. EU sizes run roughly UK + 33: a UK 8 is usually sold as an EU 42, a UK 10 as an EU 44–45. US men's sizes run about UK + 0.5 to 1: a UK 8 is a US men's 8.5 or 9. US women's sizes run about UK + 2: a UK 6 is a US women's 8. Clothing sizes: inches, centimetres and letters

Tailored clothing is at least honest about its basis: men's chest and waist sizes are measurements. In the US and UK they are given in inches — a 40-inch chest, a 32-inch waist. When a shop provides actual garment measurements in centimetres or inches, trust those over any letter or number.

The only reliable method

For shoes, skip the conversion charts and measure. Stand on a sheet of paper in the evening (feet swell slightly during the day), with your weight on the foot, and mark heel and longest toe. Measure that length in centimetres — do both feet and use the larger. Then find the brand's own size chart, which maps centimetres to its sizes, and order by the centimetre figure, adding roughly half a centimetre of growing or comfort room for trainers and boots. This works because centimetres describe your foot; sizes only describe a manufacturer's opinion about your foot.

Before you order internationally

Check the brand's own chart on the product page rather than a generic one, read recent reviews for "runs small / runs large" warnings, confirm whether the listing uses men's or women's sizing, and check the returns policy before paying — international return shipping can cost more than the shoes. Sizes will probably never be standardised worldwide, but with a tape measure, the rough rules above and the brand's chart, you rarely need to guess.

Time Units Explained: From Seconds to Centuries

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Time

Time is the unit system everyone uses and nobody designed. Almost every other measurement went decimal centuries ago, but time still runs on a Babylonian-flavoured chain of sixties, twenty-fours and sevens — with irregular months bolted on. Yet the conversions are used constantly: in payroll, in sport, in computing, in planning anything at all.

The familiar chain

  • 60 seconds = 1 minute
  • 60 minutes = 1 hour
  • 24 hours = 1 day
  • 7 days = 1 week
  • About 4.345 weeks = 1 average month (a year of 365.25 days ÷ 12 months ÷ 7 days)
  • 365.25 days = 1 average year — the quarter day is why leap years exist, adding a 29 February roughly every four years to keep the calendar in step with the seasons (with fine print: century years skip the leap day unless divisible by 400, so 2000 was a leap year but 1900 was not)

The 60s are a inheritance from Babylonian mathematics, which counted in base 60; the 24-hour day comes from Egyptian practice; the 7-day week from a blend of Babylonian and Jewish tradition that Rome spread and the world kept. Our time converter handles the whole chain, from milliseconds to years, using the 365.25-day average year.

Bigger units

Above the year, the units turn round and simple: a decade is 10 years, a century 100 years, a millennium 1,000 years. English keeps a few charming extras — a fortnight is two weeks (14 nights, in the old counting) — and science reaches for kiloyears and megayears when discussing geology and evolution. Because these larger units are defined in years, and years vary in length, they are units of convenience rather than precision: a century is exactly 100 calendar years, but its length in days depends on how many leap days it contains.

Smaller units: milliseconds and microseconds

Below the second, time goes properly decimal. A millisecond (ms) is a thousandth of a second; a microsecond (µs) is a millionth; a nanosecond (ns) a billionth. These are not abstractions. Milliseconds rule computing: a screen refreshing at 60 frames per second gives each frame 16.7 ms, a good internet ping is 10–30 ms, and a human blink lasts 100–400 ms. Microseconds matter in electronics and high-frequency trading, where a microsecond of delay is money. In sport, the second is sliced publicly: Olympic sprints are timed to the hundredth, and photo-finishes are read to the thousandth — the difference between gold and silver is often smaller than the time it takes light to cross the stadium, a comparison physicists enjoy because light travels about 30 centimetres in a single nanosecond.

Hours and minutes at work

The most practical time conversion is the payroll one. Timesheets and pay systems usually want decimal hours, while humans record hours and minutes, and the bridge is simply ×60 or ÷60:

  • 7 hours 30 minutes = 7.5 hours = 450 minutes.
  • 45 minutes = 0.75 hours; 20 minutes = 0.33 hours; 15 minutes = 0.25 hours.
  • A 37.5-hour working week = 2,250 minutes.
  • An employee working 6 hours 45 minutes of overtime has worked 6.75 hours — at time-and-a-half, paid as 10.125 hours.

The classic payroll error is writing 7 hours 30 minutes as "7.30 hours" — that figure is actually 7 hours 18 minutes, and the mistake quietly steals 12 minutes. Whenever a decimal hour appears, check it: the digits after the point are sixtieths in disguise only if you converted properly.

Converting with confidence

Time conversions reward the same discipline as any other: multiply going down the chain (hours to minutes to seconds — the numbers grow), divide going up, and sanity-check against anchors you know — a film is about 5,400–7,200 seconds, a workday 28,800 seconds, a year about 31.6 million seconds. For exact figures at any scale, the time converter at the top of this site covers milliseconds through years in both directions.

Square Feet to Square Metres: Converting Home Sizes

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Area

A flat advertised at 1,000 square feet in New York and one advertised at 93 square metres in Berlin are, it turns out, the same size. Home sizes are the area conversion most people actually need — when moving abroad, comparing listings, ordering flooring or just trying to picture a room described in the other system. This guide gives the exact factor, a mental method that is close enough for house-hunting, and a warning about what listings in different countries are actually counting.

The exact conversion

One square foot equals 0.092903 square metres. That figure is not arbitrary: a foot is exactly 0.3048 metres, and area means squaring it — 0.3048 × 0.3048 = 0.09290304. In reverse, one square metre equals 10.7639 square feet. To convert square feet to square metres, multiply by 0.092903 (or divide by 10.7639). To go back, multiply square metres by 10.7639. The area converter on this site does both, alongside acres, hectares and square yards.

The mental approach

Nobody multiplies by 0.092903 in their head while standing in a viewing. Use one of these instead. Divide by 11, then add a little: dividing by 11 undershoots by about 2%, so add back roughly a fiftieth. For 1,000 ft²: 1,000 ÷ 11 ≈ 91, plus a bit ≈ 93 m² — spot on. Or simply multiply by 0.093 if a rough figure will do: 750 ft² × 0.093 ≈ 70 m². Going the other way, multiply square metres by 11 and subtract a little: 80 m² × 11 = 880, minus a bit ≈ 861 ft² (exact: 861.1). For comparing homes, errors of a percent or two are meaningless next to layout, light and location.

Worked examples

  • 500 ft² ≈ 46 m² — a studio or small one-bedroom flat (exact: 46.45 m²).
  • 750 ft² ≈ 70 m² — a comfortable two-bedroom flat (exact: 69.68 m²).
  • 1,000 ft² ≈ 93 m² — a small family house or large flat (exact: 92.90 m²).
  • 1,500 ft² ≈ 139 m² — a typical suburban family home (exact: 139.35 m²).
  • 2,000 ft² ≈ 186 m² — a large house (exact: 185.81 m²).

A single room works the same way: a 12 ft × 15 ft bedroom is 180 ft², which is 16.7 m² — useful when a flooring or carpet quote is priced per square metre and the room was measured in feet.

What counts as "the size" differs by country

Here is the trap that catches movers out. The conversion factor is universal, but the thing being measured is not. Some listings quote only internal living space; others include the thickness of the walls, which can add 5–10%. Balconies and terraces may be counted in full, at half value, or not at all. Some countries' headline figures include a share of the building's common areas — stairs, corridors, lobby — so two identical flats can be advertised at noticeably different sizes in different systems of counting. India distinguishes "carpet area" from "built-up area" for exactly this reason; parts of Europe have similar conventions, and US listings vary by state and by whether a basement or garage is included.

The practical rule: convert the number, then ask what it counts. If a foreign listing looks 15% bigger than an equivalent local one, the difference is more likely definitions than floor space. When it matters — signing a lease, buying, ordering materials — work from room-by-room measurements rather than the headline figure. For plots of land rather than floor space, the units change again, to the hectares and acres covered in our hectares and acres guide.

One last check

Square conversions are where the most common area mistake lives: using the linear factor. A metre is 3.28 feet, but a square metre is 10.76 square feet, not 3.28 — the factor must be squared along with the unit, a point we return to in common conversion mistakes. Remember "a square metre is nearly eleven square feet" and you will never be far wrong.

Why the USA Still Uses Imperial Units

UnitConverterHub · 7 min read · History

The United States is the world's largest economy, a leader in science and technology — and one of only a handful of countries where road signs speak miles, weather comes in Fahrenheit and milk is sold by the gallon. Visitors often assume the reason is simple stubbornness. The real story is more interesting: America inherited its units, repeatedly chose not to pay the price of changing them, and quietly built its whole system on metric foundations anyway.

An inheritance, kept

The thirteen colonies used the measures of Britain, the country they traded with and were governed by. When independence came in 1776, the new United States kept the system everyone knew — the inch, foot, pound and gallon of everyday British practice. The twist is that Britain then reformed its own measures, creating the imperial system in 1824 with a new, larger gallon, while America kept the older versions. That is why the US system is technically called US customary units, not imperial: the names match, most sizes match, but the US gallon (3.785 litres) and the imperial gallon (4.546 litres) parted company two centuries ago — a divergence explored in our article on litres and gallons. The full background is in our history of measurement units.

Metric was always legal — and eventually foundational

America did not reject the metric system so much as repeatedly decline to mandate it. Thomas Jefferson studied the French proposals in the 1790s. The Metric Act of 1866 made metric units legal for use in the US. Then came the quiet turning point: in 1893, the Mendenhall Order defined every US customary unit in metric terms. From that date, the inch has legally been 2.54 centimetres, the pound 0.45359237 kilograms — American units are metric definitions wearing older names. The comparison in our metric vs imperial guide is therefore, in the US case, a comparison between metric and metric-defined units.

The 1975 law that asked nicely

The closest the US came to switching was the Metric Conversion Act of 1975, which declared metric the "preferred" system — but made conversion entirely voluntary and set no deadline. A Metric Board was created to coordinate the change and abolished a few years later. Voluntary conversion produced exactly what you would expect: industries that gained from metric (cars, pharmaceuticals, electronics) converted, and everything woven into daily infrastructure did not. Compare Britain, which began metrication in 1965 with legal compulsion in shops, yet still keeps miles and pints where the public dug in.

The real obstacle: cost and disruption

Switching is not a matter of printing new textbooks. It means replacing millions of road signs and every speedometer's emphasis, retooling factories built around inch-based machinery and fasteners, retraining the construction trades — where the 2×4, the 4×8 sheet of plywood and the 16-inch stud spacing are load-bearing habits — and re-teaching 300 million people's intuition for distance, temperature and quantity. The benefits are real but diffuse and long-term; the costs are concentrated and immediate. Every administration that has run the numbers has reached the same conclusion. One fossil of the attempt survives: Interstate 19 in Arizona, signed in kilometres as a 1970s pilot project, still measures its exits in metric.

The metric America inside

Look closer and much of the US already runs metric. Science and medicine are thoroughly metric — drugs are dosed in milligrams, laboratories in millilitres. The military measures distance in kilometres ("klicks") and coordinates on metric grids. Cars are engineered in millimetres, soda comes in 2-litre bottles, wine in 750 ml, nutrition labels in grams, and Olympic sport in metres. What remains customary is the layer the public touches directly: roads, weather, body weight, cooking and construction. It is less a country that refused metric than a country that metricated from the inside out and stopped at the skin.

When mixing goes wrong

The dual system has a genuine cost, paid occasionally in spectacular fashion. In 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter burned up in the Martian atmosphere because one engineering team had supplied thrust figures in pound-force seconds while another expected newton seconds — a $125 million lesson in labelling your units. That story, and a checklist for avoiding the same class of error at human scale, is in our guide to common conversion mistakes. Until or unless the US completes the switch, fluency in both systems remains the practical answer — which is, of course, why converter tools like this one exist.

Common Conversion Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

UnitConverterHub · 7 min read · How-to

Most conversion errors are not arithmetic errors. The multiplication is usually fine; what goes wrong is everything around it — the wrong variant of a unit, a factor that needed squaring, an offset that was forgotten. These mistakes have ruined recipes, confused download estimates and, on two famous occasions, destroyed a spacecraft and nearly an airliner. Here are the classics, and a short checklist that prevents nearly all of them.

1. Confusing US and imperial gallons

A gallon is not a gallon. The US gallon is 3.78541 litres; the imperial gallon is 4.54609 litres — about 20% more. Use the wrong one and every downstream number inherits a 20% error: fuel economy figures look impossibly good, a fish tank's volume is overstated, a recipe scaled by gallons goes sideways. The same trap exists for pints (473 ml US vs 568 ml imperial) and, in the other direction, tons (US short ton 907 kg, imperial long ton 1,016 kg, metric tonne 1,000 kg). Our litres and gallons article explains how the split happened; the defensive habit is simply to ask "which gallon?" before converting. See also fuel economy explained, where this mistake does the most damage.

2. Mixing up bits and bytes

Internet speeds are sold in megabits per second; file sizes are measured in megabytes. A byte is eight bits, so a 100 Mbps connection downloads at most 12.5 megabytes per second. People who miss the factor of eight conclude their provider is cheating them; people who miss it the other way promise a file will arrive in a minute and wait eight. Capitalisation is the tell: Mb is megabits, MB is megabytes. Our data sizes guide covers this in full.

3. Forgetting to square (or cube) the factor

The most mathematical mistake on this list. Converting area with a length factor: a metre is 3.281 feet, so the hurried conclusion is that a square metre is 3.281 square feet. It is not — area scales with the square of the factor: 3.281² = 10.764 square feet in a square metre. Volume cubes it: a cubic metre is 35.31 cubic feet, not 3.281. Flooring orders, paint coverage and land areas all fail this way. Our square feet to square metres guide shows the working.

4. Treating temperature like everything else

Temperature is the exception to the multiply-only rule, because its scales have different zero points. Converting 20 °C by multiplying alone gives nonsense; the formula needs the offset too: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. The subtler version of the mistake: temperature differences convert by multiplication only (a rise of 10 °C is a rise of 18 °F), while temperature values need the full formula. Mixing the two produces weather forecasts and oven settings that are wrong by exactly 32 degrees' worth. The full explanation is in our temperature conversion guide.

5. Rounding too early

Round an intermediate step and the error compounds through every step after it. Convert 26.2 miles using 1.6 instead of 1.609344 and you get 41.92 km instead of 42.195 km — fine for a fun run, wrong for a record. Chain two or three rounded conversions (stones to pounds to kilograms, each rounded) and the drift grows. Keep full precision until the final answer, then round once, to a sensible number of digits. False precision is the opposite sin: an answer of 154.323583 lb for a person's weight pretends to knowledge no bathroom scale possesses.

When it really mattered

The Mars Climate Orbiter (1999). A contractor's software reported thruster impulses in pound-force seconds; NASA's navigation expected newton seconds. The factor between them is about 4.45, the error went undetected through the cruise to Mars, and the $125 million spacecraft dipped too low into the atmosphere and was lost. Every figure was "correct" — in its own unit.

The Gimli Glider (1983). During Canada's transition to metric, an Air Canada 767's fuel was calculated in pounds instead of kilograms. The aircraft took off with less than half the fuel its crew believed it had, ran dry at 41,000 feet, and glided to a former air force base at Gimli, Manitoba. Remarkably, nobody was killed — but a brand-new aircraft type nearly was, over a kg-versus-lb slip. Our kilograms to pounds guide exists partly because this pair still causes trouble.

The checklist

  1. Write the unit at every step. A number without a unit is an accident waiting to happen — the Orbiter's data files were full of them.
  2. Check the variant. Which gallon, which ton, which ounce (mass or fluid)?
  3. Square areas, cube volumes. The factor gets the same power as the unit.
  4. Temperature gets its offset. Multiply and add or subtract.
  5. Round once, at the end.
  6. Sanity-check the size. Going to a smaller unit must make the number bigger. A person is not 4,500 kg; a room is not 3 m². The method in how to convert units builds this in as a step.
  7. Verify with the converter. Do it by hand, then check against the unit converter — disagreement means one of you dropped a factor, and it is cheaper to find out now.

Converting Units for Science Class and Homework

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Education

Sooner or later every science student meets the same wall: the question is in kilometres per hour, the formula wants metres per second, and the marks are for "working shown". Teachers are not being cruel — unit conversion is a real scientific skill, and there is a systematic method that makes it nearly impossible to get wrong. It is called the factor-label method, and once it clicks, homework conversions become the easiest marks on the paper.

The factor-label method, step by step

The idea is to treat units as things that can cancel, like algebraic symbols. A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1 — for example, 1,000 m / 1 km, since the top and bottom are the same length. Multiplying by 1 changes nothing about the quantity, only about how it is expressed. Chain the fractions so that unwanted units cancel, and the unit you want is the only one left standing.

Worked example: convert 90 km/h into m/s.

  1. Write the value with its units: 90 km/h, i.e. 90 km / 1 h.
  2. Kill the kilometres: multiply by (1,000 m / 1 km). The km cancels: 90,000 m / 1 h.
  3. Kill the hours: multiply by (1 h / 3,600 s). The h cancels: 90,000 m / 3,600 s.
  4. Do the arithmetic: 90,000 ÷ 3,600 = 25 m/s.
  5. Check the leftovers: metres over seconds — exactly the unit requested. If the units had not cancelled to m/s, the setup would be wrong no matter what the number said.

A simpler one: 250 cm into metres is 250 cm × (1 m / 100 cm) = 2.5 m — the cm cancels top and bottom. This is the same multiply-or-divide logic as our general guide to how to convert units, written out formally so that the units themselves check your work.

The seven SI base units

All of science is built from seven base units, and homework loves asking for them:

  • Metre (m) — length
  • Kilogram (kg) — mass (note: unusually, the base unit carries a prefix)
  • Second (s) — time
  • Ampere (A) — electric current
  • Kelvin (K) — temperature
  • Mole (mol) — amount of substance
  • Candela (cd) — luminous intensity

Everything else is derived from these: speed is metres per second, force is the newton (kg·m/s²), energy the joule, power the watt. Converting into SI base units before substituting into a formula is the habit that prevents most homework disasters.

The conversions school actually sets

  • cm ↔ m: divide or multiply by 100. (175 cm = 1.75 m.)
  • mm ↔ m: by 1,000. km ↔ m: by 1,000 the other way.
  • g ↔ kg: by 1,000. (450 g = 0.45 kg — the single most common slip is leaving masses in grams for a formula that wants kilograms.)
  • mL ↔ L: by 1,000. Also remember 1 mL = 1 cm³, which links volume and length questions.
  • km/h ↔ m/s: divide by 3.6 to go to m/s, multiply by 3.6 to come back. (36 km/h = 10 m/s is the anchor worth memorising.)
  • minutes ↔ seconds, hours ↔ seconds: by 60, and by 3,600.

Significant figures and showing your working

Two habits earn marks beyond the answer itself. First, significant figures: your answer cannot be more precise than your data. If a length is given as 2.5 m, a calculated area is 6.3 m², not 6.250000 m² — extra digits claim accuracy you do not have. Second, show the steps: write the conversion fractions, show the cancellation, then the arithmetic. In most marking schemes a correct method with one arithmetic slip earns most of the marks, while a bare wrong answer earns none — and the written units let the marker (and you) see the method is sound.

Check by hand first, then verify

Use the unit converter on this site the way a teacher would want you to: do the question by the factor-label method first, then check your answer. If the two disagree, do not just copy the converter — find which step differs. Nine times out of ten it is a fraction written upside down or a ×60 where a ÷60 belonged, and spotting it yourself is exactly the practice that makes the exam version easy.

Power Units Explained: Watts, Horsepower and BTU

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Power

A kettle is rated in watts, a car in horsepower, and an air conditioner in BTU per hour — three units for what is, underneath, one idea: how fast energy is being delivered. Power units cause a special confusion because one of the three is not really a power unit at all. Here is the whole family, untangled.

The watt: power's home unit

The watt (W) is the SI unit of power, defined as one joule of energy per second. A kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts. Wattage is a rate, not an amount — a 2,000 W kettle does not "contain" 2,000 of anything; it converts electrical energy to heat at 2,000 joules every second it runs, which is why running it for an hour uses 2 kWh (kilowatt-hours) of energy on your bill. Everyday anchors: an LED bulb draws about 10 W, a laptop 50–100 W, a microwave around 1,000 W, and an electric kettle 2,000–3,000 W — kettles are among the hungriest appliances in the house, which is why they boil so fast.

Horsepower: two flavours

The mechanical horsepower used in the US and UK is defined as 550 foot-pounds per second, which works out to 745.7 watts. Europe's metric horsepower — PS in German, cv in French and Spanish — is slightly smaller at 735.5 watts, so the same car advertised at 150 PS is 147.9 hp in American terms. The bridge figures worth keeping: 1 kW = 1.341 hp, and 1 hp = 0.746 kW. A small family car makes about 100–150 hp (75–112 kW); a hot hatch around 300 hp (224 kW); and electric cars, quoted natively in kW, slot straight into the same scale once you have the 1.341 factor.

James Watt and the original marketing unit

Horsepower was invented as advertising. In the 1780s, James Watt needed to sell steam engines to mine owners and brewers who thought in horses — the animals his engines would replace. He measured a strong horse turning a mill wheel, calculated its rate of work, and then — by most accounts — rounded his unit generously upward, defining one horsepower as 33,000 foot-pounds per minute, so that no customer could ever complain an engine underperformed the horses it replaced. It worked: "horsepower" sold engines for two centuries and outlived the working horse entirely. The watt, named in his honour later, is the tidier unit; the horsepower is the better story.

BTU: an energy unit doing a power unit's job

The British thermal unit (BTU) is properly a unit of energy: the heat needed to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit, equal to about 1,055 joules. Air conditioners, furnaces and water heaters are rated in BTU per hour — energy per time, which makes it a power rating in disguise. One watt equals about 3.412 BTU/h. The AC industry's favourite unit, the "ton" of refrigeration, is 12,000 BTU/h, about 3.517 kW — a fossil from the ice trade, being the cooling delivered by melting one US ton of ice over 24 hours. A small room air conditioner is typically 5,000–8,000 BTU/h (1.5–2.3 kW of cooling); a whole-house system runs 24,000–48,000 BTU/h, sold as "2-ton" to "4-ton" units. Note that cooling capacity is not electricity consumption: a unit moving 3.5 kW of heat may draw only about 1 kW of electricity, which is why efficiency ratings exist.

Putting the three together

  • 1 hp = 745.7 W; 1 PS = 735.5 W; 1 kW = 1.341 hp.
  • 1 BTU ≈ 1,055 J (energy); 1 kW ≈ 3,412 BTU/h (power).
  • 1 ton of refrigeration = 12,000 BTU/h ≈ 3.517 kW.
  • A 150 hp car ≈ 112 kW; a 3 kW kettle ≈ 4 hp, if you ever need your kettle in horsepower.

Power is not one of the categories in the converter at the top of this site — it converts length, weight, temperature, volume, area, speed, time, data and pressure — but the factors above, plus the habit of asking "energy, or energy per time?", will carry you through any watt, horsepower or BTU figure you meet. That question, more than any factor, is the one that prevents the classic mix-up between what a device uses and how fast it uses it.

Litres and Gallons Explained: Why US and UK Gallons Differ

UnitConverterHub · 6 min read · Volume

Ask for a gallon of fuel in London and you receive about twenty percent more than you would in New York. The gallon is two different units wearing one name — the single most expensive naming collision in everyday measurement, responsible for mis-scaled recipes, misleading fuel economy comparisons and many confused aquarium purchases. This is how it happened, and how to stay safe.

The two gallons

  • 1 US gallon = 3.78541 litres (exactly 3.785411784, being defined as 231 cubic inches).
  • 1 imperial (UK) gallon = 4.54609 litres (defined as exactly 4.54609).
  • The imperial gallon is about 20% larger: 4.54609 ÷ 3.78541 = 1.201. Turned around, a US gallon is about 0.833 imperial gallons.

Our volume converter lists both gallons separately — "Gallons (US)" and "Gallons (UK / imperial)" — precisely because treating them as one unit is the classic error described in common conversion mistakes.

How the split happened

Before standardisation, Britain had several gallons for different goods: a wine gallon of 231 cubic inches, a larger ale gallon, a corn gallon for dry measure. The American colonies used the wine gallon for liquids, and after independence the young United States simply kept it. Britain, meanwhile, tidied up: the Weights and Measures Act of 1824 abolished the old crowd of gallons and created a single imperial gallon, defined as the volume of ten pounds of water — the 4.54609-litre unit the British Empire then carried around the world. America, no longer part of that system, never adopted it. Two English-speaking worlds, two gallons, and the difference has now survived two centuries.

Pints differ too

The split runs all the way down. An imperial pint is 568 ml — 20 imperial fluid ounces — the generous measure of British pub law, summed up in the old line that "a pint of pure water weighs a pound and a quarter". A US pint is 473 ml — 16 US fluid ounces. Even the fluid ounces differ slightly (28.41 ml imperial vs 29.57 ml US), but the imperial system packs twenty of its smaller ounces into the pint against America's sixteen larger ones, so the imperial pint wins by about 20% — the same gap as the gallon, as it must be, since both pints are an eighth of their gallon. Quarts and gills scale along with their systems.

Where the difference actually matters

Fuel economy is the big one: because the imperial gallon is bigger, identical cars earn MPG figures about 20% higher in Britain than in America. Comparing a UK review's 48 MPG with a US review's 40 MPG without converting is comparing different units — our fuel economy guide gives the formulas that translate properly. Recipes and brewing come next: older British cookbooks and modern American ones both say "gallon" and "pint" while meaning different amounts, a real issue for stock, brining and home-brewing at scale. Tanks and aquariums are the third trap: the aquarium trade often quotes US gallons, so a "200-gallon tank" bought across systems can be a fifth smaller than expected — significant for stocking levels and medication doses. Paint, fuel cans, water storage and chemical dilution ratios round out the list of places a 20% error is not a rounding error.

Quick conversions to keep

  • US gallons → litres: × 3.785 (mental version: × 3.8).
  • Imperial gallons → litres: × 4.546 (mental version: × 4.5).
  • Litres → US gallons: ÷ 3.785, or × 0.264. A 50-litre tank is 13.2 US gallons.
  • Litres → imperial gallons: ÷ 4.546, or × 0.220. The same 50-litre tank is 11.0 imperial gallons.

The defensive habit costs nothing: whenever a figure says "gallon" or "pint", ask which system printed it. The litre, happily, is the same everywhere — one more reason the volume converter uses it as its base, and one more entry in the long story of how the world's units diverged, told in our history of measurement.
